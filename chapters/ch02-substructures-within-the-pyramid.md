# The Substructures Within the Pyramid

> Source: *The Minto Pyramid Principle* by Barbara Minto, OCR pages 32-40.

## Core Idea

A pyramid is built through three linked substructures: the **vertical question/answer relationship**, the **horizontal deductive or inductive relationship**, and the **narrative flow of the introduction**. Together they help the writer discover ideas, test their logic, and ensure every point answers a question relevant to the reader.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Vertical Relationship

**When to use:** When developing or testing the levels of a pyramid and deciding what information belongs beneath a point.

**How to apply it:**

1. State an idea that tells the reader something not already known.
2. Identify the logical question it raises—typically *Why?*, *How?*, or *Why do you say that?*
3. Answer that question with the points grouped horizontally on the level below.
4. Test each lower-level point for the further question it raises.
5. Continue downward until the reader should have no remaining logical questions.
6. Return to the **Key Line** and develop the next branch answering the original top-level question.

**Why it works:** It creates a controlled question/answer dialogue. Information appears when the reader needs it, making the reasoning easy to follow even when the reader disagrees with the conclusion.

**Failure mode:** Presenting information before it answers a question the reader has raised—for example, placing “Our Assumptions” before the main points—forces the reader to retain, reread, or encounter the same material again later.

### The Horizontal Relationship

**When to use:** When selecting and ordering the points that sit together beneath one idea.

**How to apply it:**

1. Confirm that every point in the grouping answers the question raised by the point above.
2. Choose one logical form for the grouping:
   - **Deductive grouping**, or
   - **Inductive grouping**.
3. Do not combine deductive and inductive logic within the same grouping.
4. Summarize the completed grouping at the next higher level of abstraction.

**Why it works:** Vertical relevance alone is insufficient; the answers must also form a recognizable logical argument. Mixing logical forms makes the grouping difficult to summarize and obscures how its points support the idea above.

#### Deductive Grouping

**When to use:** When the answer should unfold through successive reasoning steps.

**Criteria and steps:**

1. Make a statement about an existing situation.
2. Make a second statement that comments on the subject or predicate of the first.
3. State the implication of the first two conditions existing together—the “therefore” point.
4. Summarize upward, placing primary weight on the final implication.

**Canonical form:** A condition exists; a related condition also exists; therefore a conclusion follows.

#### Inductive Grouping

**When to use:** When several logically similar observations, reasons, steps, or problems jointly support an inference.

**Criteria and steps:**

1. Assemble ideas that are alike in a meaningful way.
2. Verify that one plural noun can describe them all, such as “reasons,” “problems,” or “steps.”
3. Determine what is fundamentally the same about the points.
4. Draw an inference that summarizes that common meaning at the level above.

### The Introductory Flow

**When to use:** When identifying the reader’s beginning question and establishing the top point of the document.

**How to apply it:**

1. Establish the **Situation**—the time, place, or accepted state of affairs the reader already knows.
2. Introduce the **Complication**—an event or development that changes that situation.
3. Identify the **Question** the complication raises, or would raise if the reader considered it.
4. State the document’s **Answer**, which becomes the point at the top of the pyramid.
5. Continue into the body by answering the new question raised by that top point.

This **Situation, Complication, Question, Answer** narrative puts writer and reader in the same starting position and ensures the document addresses an actual need rather than merely presenting available information.

## Key Concepts

- **Idea:** A statement that tells the reader something not known and therefore raises a logical question.
- **Pyramid:** A hierarchy of ideas under one governing thought, presented from the top downward.
- **Key Line:** The principal points immediately beneath the top idea; each begins a major branch of reasoning.
- **Vertical question/answer dialogue:** The relationship in which a lower level answers the question raised by the level above.
- **Horizontal logic:** The deductive or inductive relationship among points at the same level.
- **Deduction:** Successive reasoning from a situation and a related statement to their implication.
- **Induction:** An inference drawn from several ideas that are logically alike.
- **Plural-noun test:** A check that inductive points can all be classified as the same kind of thing.
- **Reader’s beginning question:** The pre-existing question that makes the document relevant.
- **Narrative introduction:** The Situation–Complication–Question–Answer sequence that traces the origin of that question.

## Mental Models

- **Use the vertical relationship when deciding what belongs below a point:** each lower level must answer the question the point raises.
- **Use deduction when the reasoning depends on successive statements leading to a “therefore.”**
- **Use induction when several comparable items support one broader inference.**
- **Think of the introduction as reconstructing the path by which the reader arrived at the document’s governing question.**

## Anti-patterns

- **Answering an unraised question:** Preliminary assumptions or details lack context and may need to be repeated when they finally become relevant.
- **Raising a question without answering it:** The reader cannot complete the intended reasoning path.
- **Mixing deduction and induction in one grouping:** The points no longer form one clear logical relationship.
- **Grouping merely related information:** Horizontal points must answer the same question, not simply concern the same general subject.
- **Starting with the writer’s purpose rather than the reader’s problem:** A list of topics may describe the document but does not establish why the reader should care.
- **Stopping at vague labels:** Headings such as “issues” or “considerations” do not demonstrate the inference or implication the grouped ideas support.
- **Beginning to arrange ideas before discovering the governing question:** The resulting material may be logical in isolation but irrelevant to the reader’s need.

## Worked Example

### Before: Topic list without narrative logic

A memorandum says it will discuss:

- Board composition
- Responsibilities of the Board and Executive Committee
- Participation by outside members
- Selection and tenure
- Possible operating arrangements

The list identifies subjects but leaves the reader to infer the problem and intended recommendation.

### After: Situation–Complication–Question–Answer

**Situation:** A recent reorganization transferred day-to-day responsibility for the operating units to their managers.

**Complication:** The Board has historically concentrated on short-term operating matters and is not yet organized to focus effectively on long-range policy and strategy.

**Question:** What changes will enable the Board to assume that role?

**Answer:** The Board should:

1. Transfer routine operating responsibility to the Executive Committee.
2. Broaden its composition with outside members.
3. Formalize its internal policies and procedures.

The three recommendations form the **Key Line**. Each should then be developed vertically by answering the reader’s next question: *Why is this change necessary, or how should it be carried out?*

## Key Takeaways

- Begin with a question that already exists—or should exist—in the reader’s mind.
- Make the top point the direct answer to that question.
- Require every lower-level grouping to answer the question raised by the point above.
- Use either deductive or inductive logic within a grouping, never both at once.
- Test induction with a common plural noun; test deduction for a valid “therefore.”
- Present information only when the reader’s progression through the argument calls for it.
- Stop descending when the reasoning is clear and no further logical questions need answering.
