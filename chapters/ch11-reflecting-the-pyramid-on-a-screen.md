# Reflecting the Pyramid on a Screen

> Source: *The Minto Pyramid Principle* by Barbara Minto, OCR pages 208-221.

## Core Idea

A visual presentation is not a report transferred onto slides; it is a live “show” in which the speaker is the star and the slides are visual aids. Convert the pyramid into a script and storyboard, using brief text slides to emphasize structure and exhibits to reveal relationships.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Designing Text Slides

**When to use:** To clarify the presentation’s structure or emphasize major conclusions, recommendations, next steps, and other important thought groups.

**How to design them:**

1. **Present and support one idea at a time.** Combine points only when enumerating a summary or a set that later slides will develop.
2. **Use statements, not captions.** Write the conclusion—such as “Sales outlook is favorable”—rather than merely naming the topic.
3. **Keep the text brief.** Aim for no more than about six lines or 30 words per slide; split longer ideas across slides.
4. **Use simple words and numbers.** Avoid technical language, long phrases, and needless numerical precision.
5. **Make type readable.** As a guideline:
   - Farthest viewing distance in feet ÷ 32 = minimum letter height in inches.
   - Letter height in inches × 32 = farthest legible viewing distance in feet.
6. **Make the layout visually interesting.** Treat a text slide as an exhibit made with words, showing relationships among thoughts rather than stacking bullets.
7. **Use “build” slides where helpful.** Reveal components sequentially so the audience can follow the explanation without being overwhelmed.

**Why it works:** The audience sees the essential idea while listening to the fuller explanation. This preserves attention on the speaker and prevents viewers from interpreting an ambiguous topic label for themselves.

**Failure mode:** Dense, self-contained slides invite reading rather than listening. Reading them aloud becomes a “visual recitation”; saying something different from the displayed wording creates confusion.

### Designing Exhibit Slides

**When to use:** When charts, tables, graphs, or diagrams can demonstrate structures, processes, amounts, changes, distributions, or relationships more effectively than words.

**How to design them:**

1. Decide which question the exhibit must answer:
   - What are the elements?
   - How do amounts compare—to the whole, to each other, or over time?
   - What has changed, or how has it changed?
   - How are items distributed?
   - How do items co-relate?
2. State the answer—not merely the subject—as the exhibit title.
3. Choose the visual form best suited to making that answer apparent.
4. Keep the exhibit simple and readable enough to understand during a live presentation.
5. Reserve complex exhibits for the rare case where their meaning becomes clear as the speaker develops them; use no more than one or two in a presentation.

**Why it works:** A message title directs every viewer toward the intended relationship. Without it, people may focus on different aspects of the same data according to their interests and backgrounds.

### Storyboarding

**When to use:** To convert a completed pyramid into a coherent presentation of text slides, exhibits, and spoken explanation.

**How to do it:**

1. Write the introduction in full, in speaking order. Check that the opening story is complete and that its question is valid for the audience.
2. Prepare a blank storyboard and place across the tops of its frames:
   - Introduction points that need visual support
   - Key Line points
   - Points one level below the Key Line
3. Rough out how each point should be shown. Indicate data types and intended relationships even if final numbers are not yet available.
4. Script what will be said around each slide so the sequence flows as a story.
5. Complete the slide designs and have them properly produced.
6. Rehearse repeatedly.

A simple storyboard may be a landscape sheet divided into slide-sized sections. Every frame should have a sentence or verb-containing phrase at the top stating the point the slide illustrates.

## Key Concepts

- **Visual presentation:** A live communication event requiring a star, script, storyboard, visual elements, timing, pacing, and suspense—not merely slides derived from prose.
- **Speaker as star:** The speaker and message should remain the audience’s primary focus; slides support movement through the story.
- **What You Say:** The script supplies explanation, transitions, context, and supporting detail.
- **What You Show:** The screen displays only the most significant ideas and visual relationships.
- **Text slide:** A slide used chiefly to clarify structure or emphasize an important thought group.
- **Exhibit slide:** A chart, table, graph, or diagram used to make a relationship visible.
- **Message title:** A sentence or phrase containing a verb that states the conclusion demonstrated by the slide.
- **Build slide:** A slide whose parts appear one at a time in coordination with the speaker’s explanation.
- **Visual recitation:** A presentation in which dense slide text is read aloud instead of being used as visual support.
- **Exhibit-heavy mix:** The ideal cited is approximately 90% exhibits and 10% text slides.

## Mental Models

- **Use text slides when the audience needs to see the structure or retain a major conclusion.**
- **Use exhibits when the relationship cannot readily be made clear with words alone.**
- **Think of a text slide as an exhibit made from words, not as a page of prose.**
- **Think of each slide title as the answer the visual must prove.**

## Anti-patterns

- **Turning a report into slides:** Written prose does not account for live attention, distraction, pacing, or audience response.
- **Showing long lists without an insight:** Numerous ungrouped points burden the audience and fail to communicate a summarized thought.
- **Reading every word aloud:** The slide competes with the speaker and bores the audience.
- **Paraphrasing dense displayed text:** Viewers must reconcile two verbal versions simultaneously, producing confusion.
- **Using topic captions:** A title such as “Regional profits” does not tell viewers which relationship matters.
- **Making slides function as stand-alone handouts:** Adding enough detail for absent readers usually weakens the live presentation. If necessary, pair slides with an outline-form script on facing pages.
- **Using cluttered exhibits:** Time is wasted decoding the visual rather than discussing its message.
- **Using illegible slides to signal complexity:** Minto allows a deliberate, acknowledged exception, but Gene Zelazny rejects it as a design failure and recommends omitting any visual that cannot be made legible.

## Worked Example

A fictionalized reconstruction of the supply-chain example:

### Pyramid

- **Situation:** Harbor Foods has high supply-chain costs and poor order fulfillment.
- **Complication:** Changes intended to encourage larger orders have not materially improved cost or service.
- **Question:** How can the company protect financial performance?
- **Main point:** Transform the supply chain into a source of competitive advantage.
- **Key Line:**
  1. Capture immediate cost savings.
  2. Stabilize customer service.
  3. Build capabilities for longer-term improvement.
  4. Operate for continuous advantage.

### Storyboard Conversion

| Slide | Message title | Form |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **High costs and unreliable service define the current reality** | Brief text: manufacturing problems, weak processes, poor alignment |
| 2 | **Previous changes did not remove the main bottlenecks** | Diagram of small orders, processing delays, network complexity, and forecast errors |
| 3 | **The first priority is to stabilize service while reducing cost** | Text slide with the immediate program |
| 4 | **A small customer group accounts for most order value** | Concentration chart supporting focus on priority customers |
| 5 | **Continuous improvement can turn the supply chain into an advantage** | Diagram linking speed, innovation, and collaboration initiatives |

The spoken script supplies transitions and evidence; the screen retains only the conclusions and relationships needed to guide the audience.

## Key Takeaways

- Start with the pyramid, but do not reproduce it as pages of slide text.
- Separate spoken explanation from what appears on screen.
- Use message statements as titles and make each visual support that message.
- Favor exhibits over text when showing data or relationships.
- Storyboard the introduction, Key Line, and next supporting level before final production.
- Keep every slide brief, legible, and immediately understandable.
- Rehearse the complete interaction of script, slides, pacing, and builds.
