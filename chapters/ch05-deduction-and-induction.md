# Deduction and Induction: The Difference

> Source: *The Minto Pyramid Principle* by Barbara Minto, OCR pages 78-90.

## Core Idea

Ideas relate logically in only two ways: **deductively**, where statements lead to a “therefore” conclusion, or **inductively**, where similar ideas support an inference about what they have in common. Clear writing depends on identifying the correct relationship, summarizing it accurately, and presenting the top point before its support.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Deductive Reasoning

**When to use:** Use deduction to establish an implication from related statements, particularly at lower levels of the pyramid where the full argument can be absorbed without long interruptions.

**How to construct it:**

1. Make a statement about a situation that exists.
2. Make a second statement about a related situation existing at the same time.
   - It must comment on either the **subject** or the **predicate** of the first statement.
3. State the implication of the two situations existing together—the **“therefore” conclusion**.
4. Place a summary above the argument that rests heavily on its final point.

**Rules for chaining deductive arguments:**

- Missing steps are permissible when the reader is likely to recognize and accept them.
- Limit a deductive grouping to no more than **four points**.
- Do not chain more than **two “therefore” points** if the grouping must be summarized effectively.

**Why it works—and fails:** Deduction makes derivation explicit because each point grows out of the preceding one. But at high levels it becomes ponderous: the reader must retain findings, connect them to causes, and wait for the recommended action.

---

### Inductive Reasoning

**When to use:** Use induction when several ideas, facts, or events are instances of the same kind of thing and together justify an inference about their shared significance. It is generally preferable on the **Key Line**.

**How to construct and test it:**

1. Gather ideas that appear similar.
2. Find one **plural noun** that precisely describes every idea in the grouping—for example, “steps,” “schemes,” or “indicators.”
3. Remove or relocate any idea that does not fit that description.
4. Question upward from the supporting ideas: *What can be inferred specifically from these points together?*
5. Ensure the inference does not go beyond what the grouping supports.
6. Check sentence structure:
   - Hold the subject constant and vary the predicates, or
   - Hold the predicate constant and vary the subjects.

**Why it works—and fails:** Induction creates insight by identifying meaningful sameness. It fails when the plural-noun category is vague, a point is a misfit, or the inference is so broad that many unlisted ideas could support it equally well.

### Recommendation Worksheet

**When to use:** Use after problem analysis to organize the reasoning before choosing how to present it.

**Structure:**

| Findings | Conclusions | Recommendations |
|---|---|---|
| What is going wrong | What is causing it | What should be done |

**How to use it:**

1. Arrange related findings, conclusions, and recommendations across corresponding rows.
2. Recognize that a “conclusion” is simply the summary of lower-level findings; the labels mainly indicate levels of abstraction.
3. Choose the presentation:
   - Read down the columns for a deductive sequence: problem → cause → action.
   - Reorient the logic so recommendations form the Key Line, with relevant findings and conclusions beneath each recommendation.

**Failure mode:** Treating the worksheet’s analytical sequence as the required writing sequence forces readers to reenact the entire problem-solving process.

## Key Concepts

- **Pyramid relationships:** Ideas relate upward and downward through summary and support, and sideways through logical order.
- **Key Line:** The principal grouping directly beneath the top point of the pyramid.
- **Deductive relationship:** A sequence in which the second point comments on the first and the ideas lead to a “therefore” conclusion.
- **Inductive relationship:** A grouping of similar ideas followed by an inference about their common significance.
- **Plural-noun test:** The requirement that one plural noun accurately classify every point in an inductive grouping.
- **Misfit:** An idea that cannot be described by the same plural noun as the other members of its grouping.
- **Bottom-up questioning:** Testing whether the supporting points together justify the exact inference stated above them.
- **Level of abstraction:** The degree of generality of a statement relative to the ideas it summarizes.
- **Chained deductive argument:** Two or more connected deductions in which obvious intermediate steps may be omitted.
- **News:** True but unrelated facts that generate no common inference and therefore do not help explain or defend a higher point.

## Mental Models

- **Use induction on the Key Line when the reader primarily wants to know what to do.**
- **Use deduction on the Key Line when the recommendation is alien to the reader’s expectations or cannot be understood without prior explanation.**
- **Think of deduction as a line whose points derive from one another and terminate in “therefore.”**
- **Think of induction as a set whose members share a precise plural-noun classification.**

## Anti-patterns

- **Making the reader wait for the action:** Presenting problem, then cause, then recommendation delays the information the reader cares about.
- **Spreading one deductive chain across many pages:** Intervening detail destroys deduction’s immediate clarity.
- **Calling unrelated similarities deduction:** Sharing one characteristic does not mean the second statement comments on the first.
- **Overreaching from an inductive group:** A conclusion such as “management fails deliberately” cannot be inferred merely from examples of poor management.
- **Using a summary that is too abstract:** If many additional facts could fit equally well beneath the top point, that point does not summarize this grouping specifically enough.
- **Disguising causality as induction:** If one fact causes the next—such as low productivity leading to overtime and then high prices—the relationship is deductive.
- **Including true but unrelated facts:** Truth alone does not justify inclusion; points must jointly explain or defend a higher idea.
- **Allowing the reader to predict the wrong conclusion:** Withholding the top point encourages readers to project their own—and possibly different—completion of the logic.

## Worked Example

Suppose an analysis produces three aligned chains:

| Finding | Cause | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Orders are delayed | Approval passes through four levels | Delegate routine approvals |
| Customer errors recur | Account data is entered twice | Create one shared record |
| Staff time is wasted | Reports are manually reconciled | Automate reconciliation |

### Deductive presentation

1. Explain all three operational problems.
2. Explain all three causes.
3. Finally present all three actions.

The reader must remember and match each problem to its cause and then to its action.

### Inductive Key Line

**Top point:** Simplify the operating model.

- **Delegate routine approvals** because excessive approval levels delay orders.
- **Create one shared record** because duplicate entry causes recurring errors.
- **Automate reconciliation** because manual comparison wastes staff time.

The underlying reasoning is unchanged, but each action appears first with its supporting argument directly beneath it.

## Key Takeaways

1. Test every sideways grouping as either deductive or inductive.
2. In deduction, require the second point to comment on the first and lead toward “therefore.”
3. In induction, classify every point with one precise plural noun and remove misfits.
4. Test inductive conclusions from the bottom up; do not infer more than the grouped facts support.
5. Put recommendations before extended argument in most business documents.
6. Push deduction low in the pyramid, where its points remain close together.
7. State the top point first so readers know the direction of the reasoning.
