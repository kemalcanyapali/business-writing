# Defining the Problem

> Source: *The Minto Pyramid Principle* by Barbara Minto, OCR pages 141-161.

## Core Idea

A problem is the gap between an **Undesired Result (R1)** produced by an existing situation and a **Desired Result (R2)**. Defining the situation, the event that exposed the gap, and the reader’s progress toward a solution clarifies both the analysis required and the document’s governing question.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Sequential Analysis

**Use when:** Solving a problem or evaluating an opportunity in a disciplined sequence rather than gathering and analyzing everything indiscriminately.

**Steps:**

1. **Determine whether a problem or opportunity exists or is emerging.**
2. **Locate it within the system.**
3. **Identify why it exists.**
4. **Enumerate possible responses.**
5. **Select the preferred response.**

Questions 1–2 define the problem, question 3 structures the search for causes, and questions 4–5 generate and select the solution. In the final document, answers to the first two questions generally form the introduction; answers to the remaining questions supply the pyramid.

**Why it works:** It prevents premature solution generation and pre-structures the work so that analysis translates more readily into communication.

### Problem Definition Framework

**Use when:** Beginning a problem-solving assignment, preparing a proposal or report, or diagnosing why an existing document lacks a clear question and answer.

**Core elements:**

1. **Starting Point/Opening Scene:** Sketch the existing structure or process in which the problem arose.
2. **Disturbing Event:** Identify what happened—or may happen—to disrupt the stable situation.
3. **R1 (Undesired Result):** State the result the reader dislikes, fears, or cannot accept.
4. **R2 (Desired Result):** Specify the result the reader wants instead.

The framework answers:

- **What’s going on?**  
  Starting Point/Opening Scene plus Disturbing Event.
- **What don’t we like about it?**  
  R1.
- **What do we want instead?**  
  R2.

Then:

5. Identify whether a solution has already been suggested, accepted, implemented, or found ineffective.
6. Determine the question implied by that stage.
7. Check that the introduction reflects the problem definition.
8. Check that the pyramid answers the resulting question.

**Why it works:** It creates a recognizable scaffolding for exposing gaps in understanding, focusing analysis, and constructing the introduction. The details may change as evidence accumulates, but the relationship among Opening Scene, Disturbing Event, R1, and R2 remains.

**Failure mode:** Ignoring actions already taken leads to the wrong governing question—for example, asking “What should we do?” when the reader actually needs to know whether a proposed solution is right or how to implement it.

### Situation-Complication-Question

**Use when:** Converting the completed problem definition into the introduction to a proposal, report, presentation, or speech.

**How to convert:**

1. Read the problem definition from left to right and then downward.
2. Put the established structure, process, and relevant history in the **Situation**.
3. Make the last fact already known to the reader the **Complication**.
4. State the question naturally triggered by that complication.
5. Build the pyramid so its top point answers that exact question.

A simple unresolved gap becomes:

- **Situation:** Here is the current process.
- **Complication:** It produces R1 rather than R2.
- **Question:** How should we get from R1 to R2?

If a solution already exists, its status changes the Complication and therefore the Question.

## Key Concepts

- **Problem:** A gap between the result currently or prospectively produced and the result wanted.
- **Starting Point/Opening Scene:** The structure or process visible at the moment and place where the problem originated.
- **Disturbing Event:** An external, internal, or recently recognized development that threatens the existing situation or reveals a need for change.
- **External Disturbing Event:** A change originating in the environment, such as a competitor, technology, or policy shift.
- **Internal Disturbing Event:** A company-initiated change, such as adding a process, system, market, or product direction.
- **Recently Recognized Disturbing Event:** New recognition of lagging performance, weak results, or changing customer attitudes.
- **R1 (Undesired Result):** The problem, threat, or unrealized opportunity brought to the surface.
- **R2 (Desired Result):** The measurable end product or end state wanted in place of R1.
- **Layered problem:** A problem whose history includes one or more attempted solutions that created further undesired results.
- **Reader’s question:** The question determined by how far the reader has progressed toward defining, choosing, or implementing a solution.

The seven typical question states are:

1. How do we get from R1 to R2?
2. Is the proposed solution right?
3. How do we implement the accepted solution?
4. What should we do now that the solution failed?
5. Which alternative should we choose?
6. What should our objectives and strategy be?
7. Do we have a problem, and if so, how should we respond?

## Mental Models

- **Think of the Starting Point as a stage set:** show only what a reasonably informed reader must see before the action begins.
- **Use R2 as a selection criterion when several solutions are possible:** define it specifically enough to tell whether a solution achieves it.
- **Use the reader’s latest known fact as the Complication when converting the problem into an introduction.**
- **Think of problem definition as scaffolding, not a final diagnosis:** refine its contents as data improve while preserving the relationships among its elements.

## Anti-patterns

- **Going after all available data:** Vast information encourages unfocused analysis instead of locating the gap and its likely causes.
- **Manufacturing a Disturbing Event:** If the trigger is unknown, move directly from the Opening Scene to R1 rather than inventing history.
- **Writing a vague R2:** Without a specific end state, alternatives cannot be evaluated reliably.
- **Overloading the Opening Scene:** Excess detail obscures the structure or process the reader needs to visualize.
- **Ignoring prior solution attempts:** This produces an introduction and pyramid aimed at the wrong question.
- **Introducing alternatives merely to reject them:** “A and B are bad, therefore choose C” does not establish that C solves the problem. Alternatives belong in the Complication when the reader already recognizes them.
- **Treating the first problem definition as fixed:** Evidence may require restating the Disturbing Event, R1, or R2.

## Worked Example

A household-goods retailer operates three distribution centers plus rented overflow space.

### Problem definition

| Element | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Opening Scene** | Three centers were designed to serve about 500 stores using a full-line distribution model. |
| **Disturbing Event** | Store volume is growing roughly 5% annually, and 170 additional stores are planned. |
| **R1** | Existing facilities already require rented space and will run out of capacity within two years. |
| **R2** | Add sufficient capacity while minimizing capital and operating costs and preserving processing speed and the full-line model. |
| **Known alternatives** | Expand existing centers, improve material handling, use third parties, or build another center. |

### Question selection

Because several solutions are already recognized, this is not simply “How do we add capacity?” It is problem situation 5:

> **Which alternative—or sequence of alternatives—should we choose?**

### Introduction structure

- **Situation:** Current centers cannot fully support even the existing network without overflow capacity; growth and planned openings will intensify the shortage.
- **Complication:** Several expansion approaches exist, but each has a different cost and return impact.
- **Question:** What should the distribution strategy be?
- **Answer pattern:** Add capacity incrementally, combining selected facility modifications, process improvements, temporary third-party support, and later construction so that a new center is postponed as long as practical.

## Key Takeaways

- Define the gap between R1 and R2 before analyzing causes or proposing action.
- Make the Opening Scene visual, simple, and limited to the relevant structure or process.
- State R2 as a measurable end product whenever possible.
- Determine what the reader has already decided or attempted before framing the question.
- Let the problem definition drive both the analytical work and the Situation-Complication-Question introduction.
- Ensure the final pyramid answers the precise question generated by the reader’s current position.
