# Patterns

## Pyramid Structure

**When to use:** Organizing any document, section, paragraph, or presentation around one governing thought.

**How**

1. State the governing thought.
2. Build the **Key Line** of points that explain or defend it.
3. Group and summarize support beneath each point.
4. Test the **Pyramid Rules**:
   - Higher ideas summarize those below.
   - Grouped ideas are the same kind.
   - Grouped ideas follow a logical order.
5. Present top down, ensuring each level answers the question raised above.

**Trade-offs:** Structuring must precede drafting. Groups that cannot be summarized or ordered reveal incomplete thinking.

## Situation–Complication–Question–Answer

**When to use:** Opening a document or major section and establishing why the governing point matters.

**How**

1. Establish the familiar **Situation**.
2. Add the tension-creating **Complication**.
3. Identify the resulting **Question**.
4. Give the **Answer** as the top point.
5. Identify the **New Question** raised by the Answer.
6. Make the **Key Line** answer that New Question.
7. Vary the written order—standard, direct, concerned, or aggressive—while preserving the logic.

**Trade-offs:** The introduction should remind, not prove. Controversial facts, excess evidence, or multiple questions weaken the shared starting point.

## The Top-Down Approach

**When to use:** First choice when the Subject and reader’s likely Question can be reconstructed.

**How**

1. State the Subject, reader’s Question, and Answer.
2. Identify the familiar Situation and triggering Complication.
3. Reconcile Situation, Complication, Question, and Answer.
4. Ask the New Question raised by the Answer.
5. Build the Key Line deductively or inductively, then repeat downward.

**Trade-offs:** Fast and reader-centered, but unreliable if the Question or Answer is unknown. Validate the structure before drafting.

## The Bottom-Up Approach

**When to use:** When the Subject, Question, Answer, or relationships among points are unclear.

**How**

1. List candidate points, starting with recommendations where possible.
2. Remove repetition; group like points in logical order.
3. Summarize groups and draw conclusions upward.
4. Infer the governing Answer and Key Line.
5. Reconstruct Situation–Complication–Question–Answer to test relevance.
6. Communicate top down.

**Trade-offs:** Useful for discovery, but can preserve research order. A list becomes a structure only through explicit relationships and summaries.

## The Three Logical Orders

**When to use:** Arranging and testing an inductive grouping.

**How**

1. Identify the group’s analytical source.
2. Use **Time Order** for actions, processes, or cause-and-effect sequences.
3. Use **Structural Order** for parts of a real or conceptual whole; make divisions **Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive (MECE)**.
4. Use **Degree Order** to rank like items by one defining characteristic.
5. Keep points at the same abstraction level.
6. Reconstruct the underlying process, structure, or class to find omissions.

**Trade-offs:** One order must be the backbone. No defensible order usually signals a mixed, incomplete, or invalid group.

## Deductive Reasoning and Inductive Reasoning

**When to use:** Choosing the horizontal relationship among points answering the same question.

**How**

- Use one form within a grouping.
- **Deductive Reasoning:** state a condition, add a statement about its subject or predicate, then give the “therefore” implication.
- **Inductive Reasoning:** group meaningfully similar ideas, apply the plural-noun test, remove misfits, and infer their specific common significance.
- Prefer induction on the Key Line.
- Keep deduction low and compact: usually no more than four points or two “therefore” steps.

**Trade-offs:** Deduction is explicit but can become ponderous. Induction is easier to absorb but may produce vague categories or unsupported conclusions.

## Summarizing Grouped Ideas

**When to use:** Converting ordered groups into substantive higher-level ideas.

**How**

1. Replace empty labels such as “three problems” with meaningful conclusions.
2. For actions, specify each action and visualize its end product.
3. Distinguish actions done **before** another action from those done **so that** it can occur.
4. State the observable effect of the complete action set.
5. For situations, compare subjects, predicates, and implied judgments.
6. Group closest similarities, infer their shared significance, and verify MECE support.

**Trade-offs:** Specific summaries expose gaps and overlaps and may force restructuring. Visualization cannot replace missing evidence.

## Problem Definition Framework

**When to use:** Before analysis, proposal design, or reframing a report with an unclear governing question.

**How**

1. Sketch the **Starting Point/Opening Scene** and **Disturbing Event**.
2. State **R1 (Undesired Result)** and measurable **R2 (Desired Result)**.
3. Record proposed, accepted, implemented, or failed solutions.
4. Select the reader’s actual question based on current progress.
5. Convert the definition into Situation–Complication–Question.
6. Ensure the pyramid answers that exact Question.

**Trade-offs:** This is revisable scaffolding. A vague R2 blocks evaluation; ignoring prior attempts produces the wrong question.

## Diagnostic Frameworks

**When to use:** Locating a problem and its causes before broad data collection.

**How**

1. Derive the framework from the Problem Definition Framework’s Opening Scene.
2. Visualize the relevant physical, financial, task, activity, classification, or choice structure.
3. Generate possible causes through division, cause-and-effect tracing, or classification.
4. Make upper branches MECE.
5. Turn causes into yes-no questions and define required evidence.
6. Test efficiently, eliminating unlikely causes first and collecting only decision-relevant evidence.

**Trade-offs:** Focused testing saves research but requires subject knowledge. The framework guides diagnosis; it does not replace expertise.

## Logic Trees

**When to use:** After diagnosis, to decide what could and should be done.

**How**

1. Express the action objective as R2.
2. Decompose possible actions into MECE alternatives until branches are actionable.
3. Estimate benefits and risks.
4. Test combinations against R2 and select the recommended set.
5. Use the tree to detect pyramid gaps, overlaps, and irrelevant items.

**Trade-offs:** Logic trees structure alternatives but do not diagnose causes. Mechanical decomposition can suppress creative options.

## Reflecting the Pyramid on the Page, on a Screen, and in Prose

**When to use:** Translating a completed pyramid into written, visual, or spoken communication.

**How**

- **Page:** Show abstraction through headings, indentation, numbering, or displayed points. Keep same-level ideas visually and grammatically parallel; introduce groups before details and connect them with idea-based transitions.
- **Screen:** Storyboard the introduction, Key Line, and next supporting level. Use brief message titles and relational exhibits; keep spoken explanation separate.
- **Prose:** **CREATE THE IMAGE** of the nouns and their relationships, then **COPY THE IMAGE IN WORDS** using direct, ordinary language.
- Ensure format and wording reinforce rather than replace logic.

**Trade-offs:** Over-formatting creates false hierarchy, dense slides compete with the speaker, and abstract prose burdens memory. Design should follow the completed pyramid.
