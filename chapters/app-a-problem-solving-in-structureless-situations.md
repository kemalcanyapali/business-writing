# Appendix A: Problem Solving in Structureless Situations

> Source: *The Minto Pyramid Principle* by Barbara Minto, OCR pages 229-234.

## Core Idea

When the structure behind an unexpected result is absent, invisible, or inadequate, problem solving requires **Scientific Abduction**: invent a plausible explanatory structure and test its necessary consequences. Like analytical problem solving, it advances through explicit visual representations that make the next analytical step clear.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Analytical Abduction

**Use when:** An undesirable result exists and the structure that could have caused it is already known.

**How to apply:**

1. Observe the **Result**.
2. Select a known **Rule** that could explain it.
3. Identify and test the possible **Case**.
4. Retain or reject the explanation based on whether the case exists.

**Why it works:** It converts a suspected cause into a testable proposition rather than treating the first plausible explanation as fact.

### Scientific Abduction

**Use when:** You cannot explain a result because the relevant structure:

- Does not yet exist, as in invention.
- Is invisible and can be studied only through its effects.
- Fails to explain the observed result.

**How to apply:**

1. Define the discrepancy between observation and what prevailing theory predicts.
2. Examine the situation’s structural elements and current assumptions.
3. Hypothesize an alternative structure that could explain the discrepancy.
4. Derive a consequence that must follow if the hypothesis is true.
5. Devise an experiment that gives a clear yes-or-no result.
6. Recycle the process with sub-hypotheses or sequential hypotheses.
7. Reformulate the theory from the experimental findings.

**Failure mode:** Merely changing conditions to “see what happens” produces observations without a decision rule. An experiment is useful only if its outcome supports keeping or discarding a hypothesis.

### Rule–Case–Result Reasoning

Peirce’s three entities distinguish the forms of reasoning:

| Form | Starting knowledge | Inference |
|---|---|---|
| **Deduction** | Rule + Case | A necessary Result |
| **Induction** | Case + Result | A probable Rule |
| **Abduction** | Result + Rule | A possible Case to test |

Complex problem solving rotates among all three. The form being used—and the strength of its conclusion—depends on where the reasoning starts.

### Basic Process

The appendix aligns **Analytical Problem Solving** with **Scientific Problem Solving**:

| Basic question | Analytical Problem Solving | Scientific Problem Solving |
|---|---|---|
| What is the problem? | Visualize the gap between actual and desired results. | Define the gap between observation and theoretical expectation. |
| Where does it lie? | Identify structural elements that could cause the result. | State assumptions that might create the discrepancy. |
| Why does it exist? | Test which elements are causal and why. | Hypothesize alternative explanatory structures. |
| What could we do? | Formulate structural changes. | Devise experiments that exclude hypotheses. |
| What should we do? | Build the most satisfactory new structure. | Reformulate the theory from the evidence. |

## Key Concepts

- **Structureless situation:** A problem whose explanatory structure is nonexistent, invisible, or contradicted by the observed result.
- **Rule:** A belief about how the world is structured.
- **Case:** An observed fact or condition existing in the world.
- **Result:** The occurrence expected when a rule applies to a case.
- **Hypothesis:** A proposed structure capable of explaining an observed discrepancy.
- **Visual thinking:** Forming a concrete image of the problem elements, possible structure, or necessary consequence.
- **Apt analogy:** A connection between the problem and known features of the world that suggests a productive hypothesis.
- **Experiment:** A test designed to confirm or exclude a hypothesis through an unequivocal outcome.
- **Discrepancy:** The difference between what occurs and what the prevailing theory says should occur.

## Mental Models

- **Use Analytical Abduction when the causal structure is known but the applicable case is uncertain.**
- **Use Scientific Abduction when the observed result cannot be explained by an available structure.**
- **Think of a hypothesis as a structure whose unavoidable consequence can be tested.**
- **Think of each visual representation as an anchor that directs the next analysis and, later, the communication.**

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating a hypothesis as a conclusion:** Abduction yields a possibility, not a necessity; the case still requires testing.
- **Generating hypotheses without examining the situation:** Productive hypotheses should arise from structural elements, assumptions, and relevant analogies—not from arbitrary speculation.
- **Running exploratory experiments without a prediction:** “Seeing what happens” cannot conclusively retain or eliminate a hypothesis.
- **Accepting ambiguous evidence:** If several interpretations fit the outcome, the experiment has not supplied the required yes-or-no answer.
- **Ignoring prevailing assumptions:** An unexplained result may indicate that the theory’s structure, rather than the observation, needs revision.

## Worked Example

An existing theory says motion must stop when the initiating force stops, yet a launched object continues moving.

1. **Discrepancy:** Observation conflicts with the theory.
2. **Structural elements:** The object’s weight, distance traveled, and elapsed time.
3. **Competing hypotheses:** Force may relate to weight, distance, or time.
4. **Testable consequence:** If the time hypothesis is correct, distance should increase with the square of elapsed time—for example, time intervals of 1, 2, and 3 correspond to relative distances of 1, 4, and 9.
5. **Experiment:** Roll a ball down an incline to slow its fall enough to measure distance at fixed time intervals.
6. **Decision:** If the measured relationship matches the prediction, retain the hypothesis; otherwise discard it.
7. **Revised rule:** Force explains change in velocity rather than velocity itself.

## Key Takeaways

- First determine whether the explanatory structure is known or must be invented.
- Keep **Deduction**, **Induction**, and **Abduction** distinct by tracking which two of Rule, Case, and Result are known.
- Generate hypotheses from observed elements, explicit assumptions, and useful analogies.
- Translate every hypothesis into a consequence that must follow if it is true.
- Design experiments to exclude alternatives, not merely accumulate observations.
- Represent each stage clearly enough that the next step becomes visible.
