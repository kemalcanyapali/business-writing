# Fine Points of Introductions

> Source: *The Minto Pyramid Principle* by Barbara Minto, OCR pages 54-77.

## Core Idea

A good introduction tells a brief, familiar story that establishes the **Situation**, introduces a **Complication**, and leads to the single **Question** answered by the document’s main point. Its purpose is to remind the reader of relevant facts, align perspectives, and create a logical need for the answer—not to present evidence or conduct analysis.

## Frameworks Introduced

### S-C-Q Structure

**Use when:** Opening any document or major Key Line section.

**How to apply:**

1. **Situation** — Begin with a self-sufficient, noncontroversial statement about the subject. Anchor the reader in a recognizable time, place, or state of affairs.
2. **Complication** — State what happened next or what changed. It need not be a “problem”; it is the event that creates tension.
3. **Question** — Identify the one question naturally triggered by the complication.
4. **Solution/Answer** — State the main point that resolves the question.
5. In a long document, follow with Key Line points that explain the answer and preview the document’s plan.

**Why it works:** The narrative starts with facts the reader accepts, then makes the answer feel relevant and logically necessary. It also gives the reader a framework for interpreting the evidence that follows.

**Failure mode:** If the opening requires proof, contains extensive exhibits, or raises multiple unrelated questions, it is informing rather than reminding and has not established a clean logical starting point.

### Four Questions Most Documents Answer

1. **Which action is required?**
2. **How will, does, or did the action work?**
3. **Is the proposed action worth taking?**
4. **What caused the observed result?**

Determine the governing question before drafting. If two questions appear—such as “Should we enter, and if so, how?”—make their dependency explicit. A “yes” answer to the first becomes the pyramid’s main point, and “How?” is answered on the Key Line.

### Alternative Introduction Orders

Use all essential story elements, but vary their order to establish tone:

- **STANDARD: situation—complication—solution** — Neutral narrative progression.
- **DIRECT: solution—situation—complication** — Leads immediately with the recommendation.
- **CONCERNED: complication—situation—solution** — Emphasizes the seriousness of the issue.
- **AGGRESSIVE: question—situation—complication** — Challenges the reader and creates urgency before presenting the answer.

Changing order changes tone, not the underlying logic.

### Giving Direction

**Pattern:**

- **S:** We want to do X.
- **C:** We need you to do Y.
- **Q:** How should Y be done?
- **A:** The required steps.

The question may remain unstated in the document, but the writer should spell it out privately before drafting.

### Seeking Approval to Spend Money

**Pattern:**

- **S:** We have a problem.
- **C:** We have a solution that will cost money.
- **Q:** Should I approve it?
- **A:** Approve the request.

Typical supporting reasons are:

1. The problem cannot wait.
2. The proposed action will solve it—or is the best available solution.
3. Savings or returns justify the cost.
4. The action may provide additional benefits.

### Explaining “How to”

When the reader has not done the activity before:

- **S:** We must perform X.
- **C:** We are not set up to do it.
- **Q:** How do we get set up?

When an existing process performs poorly:

- **S:** Your present system is X.
- **C:** It does not work properly.
- **Q:** How should it change?

Make the current and recommended processes visible. Their differences become the Key Line steps.

### Choosing Among Alternatives

**Use when:** The alternatives are genuinely known in advance.

**Pattern:**

- **S:** We want to accomplish X.
- **C:** Several ways of doing it are available.
- **Q:** Which makes the most sense?

Prefer organizing the answer around decision criteria: one choice is faster, cheaper, or easier to implement. If no option wins on every criterion, state the decisive advantage of the chosen option and the main reason each other option was rejected. If the choice depends on desired outcomes, organize around **alternative objectives**, not supposed alternative solutions.

### Key Line Introductions

Each Key Line point should receive its own brief S-C-Q introduction:

1. Recall what the reader already knows about the new subject.
2. Connect it to the overall point or preceding section.
3. create the question answered by the Key Line point.
4. Use a heading that conveys the essence of the point.

The initial introduction establishes the document’s subject. Later introductions reflect what has just been placed in the reader’s mind and explain why the next point is relevant.

## Key Concepts

- **Situation:** An accepted, self-sufficient starting statement that places the reader within the story.
- **Complication:** The development that disturbs the situation and triggers a question.
- **Question:** The single issue the document exists to answer.
- **Solution/Answer:** The main point resolving the question.
- **Key Line:** The principal points that answer the question raised by the main point and reveal the document’s plan.
- **Reminder, not information:** Introductory material should already be known or readily accepted, not require demonstration.
- **Question provenance:** The logical path showing why this reader, in this situation, would ask this question.
- **Ideas rather than categories:** Key Line points must make assertions; labels such as “Background” merely name subjects.
- **Reader-relative length:** Introduction length depends on what the reader needs to understand the main point and want to continue.
- **Implied question:** A question that need not appear in the prose but must be explicit in the writer’s thinking.

## Mental Models

- **Use S-C-Q when the reader must understand why your answer matters.**
- **Think of an introduction as a familiar unfinished story whose resolution is your main point.**
- **Use process comparison when recommending how to repair an existing system.**
- **Think of each Key Line section as a smaller document requiring its own brief introduction.**

## Anti-patterns

- **Beginning with a controversial or dependent statement:** The reader cannot immediately understand and accept the starting point.
- **Overloading the introduction with exhibits or proof:** This signals that the writer is arguing before establishing common ground.
- **Listing topics instead of ideas:** “Background,” “principles,” and “benefits” do not communicate the document’s message or form a clear argument.
- **Repeating Key Line points as headings in a short document:** Use them directly as visible topic sentences instead.
- **Making the introduction proportional to document length:** Length should follow reader need, not page count.
- **Treating every obstacle as the Complication:** The Complication is a narrative function and may be an action, proposal, or failed result rather than a problem.
- **Presenting unrelated questions:** A document should pivot on one governing question.
- **Starting a “how to” recommendation without mapping both processes:** Important differences—and therefore necessary steps—are likely to be omitted.
- **Calling objectives “alternative solutions”:** Different options may merely serve different desired outcomes.

## Worked Example

### Before: topic-based outline

A manager proposes improving a monthly demand-planning system:

- Background
- Forecasting principles
- Scheduling
- Meeting procedures
- Benefits

This gives the reader categories but no message.

### Reasoning reconstruction

**S:** The company currently forecasts demand in June, immediately creates a six-month production schedule, and adjusts it informally at monthly meetings.  
**C:** Early commitments and unstructured revisions repeatedly produce inaccurate schedules.  
**Q:** How should the forecasting process change?  
**A:** Move forecasting later and formalize the revision process.

Compare the processes:

| Current process | Recommended process |
|---|---|
| Forecast early | Establish inventory targets first |
| Build the master schedule immediately | Delay the master schedule until better information exists |
| Revise informally in meetings | Require expert review before structured monthly adjustment |

### After: idea-based Key Line

1. **Set inventory targets to guide scheduling.**
2. **Delay the master schedule until forecasts are more reliable.**
3. **Use a formal review process for monthly revisions.**

The visible differences between present and recommended practice generate the document’s steps.

## Key Takeaways

- Establish accepted common ground before introducing disagreement or recommendation.
- Make the Complication lead naturally to one governing Question.
- State Key Line points as complete ideas that reveal the document’s message.
- Introduce each major Key Line point with a short, reader-specific S-C-Q story.
- Keep introductions only as long as needed to place writer and reader at the same starting point.
- Work backward from body points when the opening Question is difficult to identify.
- Select introduction order deliberately to create a standard, direct, concerned, or aggressive tone.
