# How to Build a Pyramid Structure

> Source: *The Minto Pyramid Principle* by Barbara Minto, OCR pages 41-53.

## Core Idea

Build the pyramid by identifying the reader’s Question and answering it at the top, then organizing the supporting ideas to answer the New Question raised by that Answer. Start with **The Top-Down Approach** whenever possible; use **The Bottom-Up Approach** when the subject, question, or answer is not yet clear.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Top-Down Approach

**When to use:** Use first in most writing situations, especially when you roughly know the subject and can reconstruct what the reader already knows and wants answered.

**How to apply:**

1. **Draw a box.** Treat it as the top of the pyramid and write the Subject being discussed.
2. **Decide the Question.** Visualize the reader and state what question the reader should have answered about the Subject.
3. **Write down the Answer.** State it if known, or establish that you can answer the Question.
4. **Identify the Situation.** Begin with a noncontroversial statement about the Subject—something the reader already knows or can readily verify.
5. **Develop the Complication.** Ask what happened within the Situation to trigger a problem, discrepancy, or concern.
6. **Recheck the Question and Answer.** The Complication should naturally raise the stated Question. If it does not, revise the Complication, Question, or both.
7. **Find the Key Line.** Ask what New Question the Answer raises and decide whether to answer it deductively or inductively. For an inductive grouping, identify the plural noun that describes all the supporting points—for example, “reasons.”
8. **Structure the support points.** Repeat the question-and-answer process at each lower level as needed.

**Why it works:** The introductory flow—**Situation, Complication, Question, Answer**—makes its elements check one another. It forces the writer to select information relevant to the reader’s actual Question rather than merely report available facts.

**Failure mode:** A document may describe procedures, findings, or background without answering the question that caused it to be written. Another common failure is to begin drafting prose before the structure is clear; once wording exists, writers become reluctant to revise even disjointed thinking.

### The Bottom-Up Approach

**When to use:** Use when the Subject is uncertain, the reader’s Question is unclear, or the writer cannot distinguish what the reader does and does not know.

**How to apply:**

1. **List all the points** you think you want to make.
2. **Work out the relationships** between them, laying out cause-and-effect or other logical connections as visibly as possible.
3. **Draw conclusions** from those relationships.
4. Use the conclusions to infer the top-level Answer and Key Line.
5. Reconstruct the introduction to test the Subject, Situation, Complication, Question, and Answer.

Start with recommendations when possible: action ideas are generally easier to test for validity than situation ideas. Check whether apparently different recommendations are actually repetitions of the same idea.

**Why it works:** Grouping and mapping the raw points exposes duplicate ideas, missing links, and separate lines of reasoning. The conclusions can then be elevated into a clear top-level message.

**Failure mode:** Merely listing observations preserves the order in which facts were discovered rather than revealing the logic among them. The result rambles and leaves the reader to derive the conclusion.

### Situation–Complication–Question–Answer

**When to use:** Use to think through and validate the introduction before structuring the body.

**Criteria:**

- **Situation:** A statement about the Subject that the reader will accept as true.
- **Complication:** The development that creates tension and triggers a question.
- **Question:** The natural question raised by that Complication.
- **Answer:** The document’s governing idea.

Think through these elements beginning with the Situation, even though they may later be written in another order to create a different tone.

### New Question and Key Line

**When to use:** Use after stating the top-level Answer.

1. Ask what New Question the Answer immediately raises.
2. Make every Key Line point the same type of answer to that question.
3. Put the points in logical order.
4. Develop each point only as far as necessary.

For example, if the New Question is “Why?”, every Key Line point must be a reason.

## Key Concepts

- **Subject:** The topic of the document and the subject of the sentence at the top of the pyramid.
- **Question:** What the reader wants answered about the Subject.
- **Answer:** The governing idea at the top of the pyramid.
- **Situation:** Familiar or objectively verifiable context that the reader will not dispute.
- **Complication:** The event, problem, or discrepancy that causes the Question to arise.
- **New Question:** The question raised in the reader’s mind by the Answer.
- **Key Line:** The main supporting ideas immediately beneath the top-level Answer.
- **Historical chronology:** A sequence of what happened; it belongs in the introduction unless analyzed as a cause-and-effect relationship.
- **Idea:** New thinking that raises a question and relates logically to other ideas.
- **Idea heading:** A heading that communicates a substantive point, rather than a category such as “Findings” or “Conclusions.”

## Mental Models

- **Use The Top-Down Approach when the reader’s Question can be reconstructed from familiar context.**
- **Use The Bottom-Up Approach when you have points but cannot yet state the governing idea.**
- **Think of the introduction as a diagnostic test:** the Complication must trigger the Question, and the Answer must resolve it.
- **Use induction at the Key Line when a deductive conclusion merely repeats the top-level message.**

## Anti-patterns

- **Drafting the entire document before structuring it:** Polished wording creates resistance to necessary structural revision.
- **Omitting the introduction:** Background then leaks into the body and can force an unnecessarily complex deductive argument.
- **Reporting findings instead of answering the Question:** The reader receives information but must infer its significance.
- **Putting simple chronology in the body:** Events are not body ideas unless their cause-and-effect relationship has been analyzed.
- **Introducing disputed or unknown information as familiar context:** This distorts the Question the reader would naturally ask.
- **Using known information as lower-level support:** This suggests that important introductory context was omitted.
- **Using category headings such as “Findings”:** They provide no scanning value and do not reveal the argument.
- **Overstructuring a deduction:** Repeating “therefore, do X” beneath an already stated recommendation adds no useful answer.

## Worked Example

A finance team is asked whether a large retailer should replace individual delivery-ticket billing with one monthly data submission and payment.

### Before: information without an answer

- Specifies required fields.
- Describes the retailer’s extraction program.
- Explains balancing procedures.
- Describes final system processing.

These points explain how the system might operate but do not answer whether the proposed change makes sense.

### Reconstructed pyramid

**Subject:** Retailer’s requested billing change.

**Situation:** The retailer has requested a different procedure for submitting delivery information and payment.

**Complication:** Management has asked whether the proposed procedure is sensible.

**Question:** Does the change make sense?

**Answer:** Yes, the change is a good idea.

**New Question:** Why?

**Key Line—Reasons:**

1. It will provide all required information.
2. It will accelerate cash receipt.
3. It will reduce internal processing work.

The procedural details now belong beneath the relevant reason rather than appearing as an undifferentiated list. The revised structure answers the decision question first and uses operational facts as support.

## Key Takeaways

1. Start top down before resorting to bottom-up sorting.
2. Identify the reader’s Question before selecting supporting information.
3. Test the introduction by ensuring the Complication naturally raises the Question.
4. Make every Key Line point answer the same New Question.
5. Put familiar history in the introduction and logical ideas in the body.
6. State the complete line of thinking early enough that the reader can grasp it quickly and scan the support.
7. Prefer idea-bearing headings and, where practical, inductive Key Line groupings.
