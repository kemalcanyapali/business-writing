# Reflecting the Pyramid on the Page

> Source: *The Minto Pyramid Principle* by Barbara Minto, OCR pages 187-207.

## Core Idea

The page should visually reproduce the pyramid’s levels and groupings so the reader can grasp the Introduction, Main Point, and Key Line points almost immediately. Formatting and transitions must reinforce—not replace—the logical relationships among ideas.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Highlight the Structure

Use when converting a completed pyramid into prose.

1. Match the format to the argument’s levels of abstraction.
2. Make ideas at the same level look alike.
3. Place subordinate ideas visually beneath or to the right of the ideas they support.
4. Introduce each grouping before displaying its parts.
5. Add transitions where the reader must move between major groups.

The main formatting options are **Hierarchical Headings**, **Underlined Points**, **Decimal Numbering**, **Indented Display**, and **Dot-Dash Outlines**.

### Hierarchical Headings

Use for longer reports in which each Key Line point requires more than a paragraph or two.

1. Put major section headings at the left margin.
2. Move subsection headings, numbered paragraphs, dash points, and dot points progressively to the right.
3. **Never use only one of any element.** A heading signals that its idea belongs to a group.
4. **Show parallel ideas in parallel form.** Apply grammatical parallelism within each group, not necessarily across different groups.
5. Limit each heading to the essence of its thought.
6. Treat headings as outside the prose: the text should still read smoothly if they are removed.
7. Introduce every group by stating both the point it supports and the ideas to come.
8. Do not subdivide unless the subdivisions genuinely help the reader.

Proper headings can turn the table of contents into a précis of the report. Generic labels such as “Background,” “Findings,” and “Recommendations” fail because they identify document categories rather than communicate actual ideas.

### Underlined Points

Use when readers should be able to scan complete support statements below the Key Line.

1. State and underline each support point in full.
2. Distinguish lower levels through indentation, numbering, capitalization, or typography.
3. Make every lower point directly answer the question raised by the point above.
4. Keep statements sparse—rethink points longer than roughly a dozen words or containing multiple subjects and predicates.
5. Limit points to the outline of the deductive or inductive argument: ordinarily no more than four in a chained deduction or five in an inductive grouping.

This format exposes weak logic quickly. Background, amplification, and verbal transitions must sit in introductory or concluding paragraphs rather than inside the outlined points.

### Decimal Numbering

Use when precise cross-reference is important.

1. Assign numbers that correspond to actual divisions of thought.
2. Prefer numbering in conjunction with hierarchical headings rather than instead of them.
3. Do not number introductions, summaries, linking comments, or introductions to subpoints.
4. Include a topic label when referring back—for example, “Section 3.2 on operating costs”—so the number carries context.

Excessive numbering interrupts concentration and makes later amendments cumbersome.

### Indented Display

Use in short documents where headings or decimal numbering would be excessive but a group still needs to stand out.

1. Introduce the overall idea.
2. Set the supporting points apart as an indented or numbered list.
3. Express all points in the same grammatical form.
4. Generally limit the technique to one displayed grouping per memorandum.

Parallel display saves words, speeds comprehension, and reveals incomplete or mismatched ideas.

### Dot-Dash Outlines

Use for a **lap visual** or progress review read by a small group while the author is present.

1. Place increasingly subordinate ideas farther to the right.
2. Make one short, direct statement at each level.
3. Omit grace notes and verbal liaisons.
4. Use parallel construction at the same level where possible.
5. Ensure that each lower-level statement directly explains or supports the level above.
6. Reveal only enough information for deliberate reading and discussion.

The rule requiring at least two points at every level is looser here, but the format should not be used unless its other disciplines are followed.

### Show Transitions Between Groups

Use in longer documents to keep the reader oriented between Key Line points and major subdivisions.

- Introduce each Key Line point.
- At major boundaries, look backward to the idea just completed and forward to the idea coming next.
- Connect what sections **say**, not merely what they **do**.
- Use **Telling a Story**, **Referencing Backward**, or—after a long section—**Summarizing Sections**.

Mechanical announcements such as “This chapter discussed X; the next discusses Y” fail because they describe document machinery rather than continue the reasoning.

### Telling a Story

Use to introduce a new Key Line point through a compact **Situation-Complication-Question** sequence.

1. Begin from the reader’s current position.
2. Use only facts the reader already knows or accepts.
3. Create a complication that raises the relevant question.
4. Present the next Key Line point as the answer.

### Referencing Backward

Use when the reader already has enough context for the next section.

1. Pick up a key word, phrase, or central idea from the preceding group.
2. Carry it into the opening sentence of the new group.
3. Connect it directly to the new section’s major point.
4. Introduce the support ideas that follow.

### Summarizing Sections

Use after an unusually long or complex section. Restate the principal matter and tone by recombining the relevant points already present in the pyramid.

### Making Full Conclusions

Use only when a longer document needs a purposeful ending.

- Do not merely restate that the report made recommendations.
- Put the message’s significance into perspective.
- Leave the reader with an appropriate desire to act.
- Offer either a broader insight or a prescription for action, with restraint appropriate to the reader and subject.

### Stating Next Steps

Use when the recommended course is likely to be accepted and immediate implementation actions are needed.

1. Create a **Next Steps** section.
2. Include only logically obvious, noncontroversial actions.
3. Move any action likely to raise “Why?” into the body, where it can be supported by the pyramid’s logic.

## Key Concepts

- **Visual hierarchy:** Page layout that mirrors the pyramid’s levels of abstraction.
- **Key Line points:** The major ideas directly supporting the document’s Main Point.
- **Parallel form:** Consistent grammatical construction for ideas belonging to one group.
- **Division of thought:** A genuine logical subdivision that warrants a heading or other visual marker.
- **Précis:** A compressed expression of the report’s reasoning, potentially formed by well-written headings.
- **Lap visual:** A dot-dash document discussed page by page with a small group.
- **Transitionary phrase:** Language that reconnects the reader’s position to the argument’s next grouping.
- **Sunset:** A centered line of asterisks signaling a concluding passage.
- **Next Steps:** Immediate, self-evident actions following acceptance of a recommendation.

## Mental Models

- Use **visual equivalence** when ideas occupy the same logical level.
- Use **increasing indentation** when ideas become increasingly subordinate.
- Think of a heading as a **signpost outside the prose**, not as a sentence carrying the argument.
- Use a transition as a **handhold that pulls the reader back to the correct place in the logic**.

## Anti-patterns

- **Decorative headings:** They imply a logical grouping that does not exist.
- **A single subsection or lone dash point:** One item cannot constitute a group at that level.
- **Generic table-of-contents labels:** They conceal the report’s actual thinking.
- **Headings that complete sentences in the text:** Readers may skip headings, breaking the prose.
- **Unintroduced lists or sections:** The reader must infer the governing point after encountering its details.
- **Mixed grammatical forms:** They obscure similarity and often hide incomplete thinking.
- **Over-formatting:** Too many levels or displayed groups weaken rather than sharpen the visual effect.
- **Numbering every paragraph:** It interrupts comprehension and creates maintenance problems.
- **Unsupported Next Steps:** Debatable actions belong in the argument, not in an implementation checklist.

## Worked Example

### Before: ideas buried in prose

A manager says that two October workshops will require introductory slides, examples of encouraging language, earlier innovation results, and material on creating an innovative environment. The requests are scattered across several paragraphs, and the last item never specifies what the slides should show.

### After: indented display

For both workshops, prepare slides showing:

1. **The main ideas in the introduction.**
2. **Examples of constructive reinforcement language.**
3. **The results achieved by earlier innovations.**
4. **The steps required to create an environment for innovation.**

The introductory sentence establishes the governing category—slides needed for both workshops. Parallel noun phrases make the four items visibly comparable, while the revised fourth point supplies the missing content exposed by the display.

## Key Takeaways

- Choose page design only after the pyramid’s logic is complete.
- Match every visual level to a real level of abstraction.
- Introduce each group before presenting its component ideas.
- Keep headings concise, parallel, and independent of the prose.
- Use transitions to connect ideas, not merely sections.
- Reserve conclusions for significance or action; reserve **Next Steps** for obvious implementation moves.
