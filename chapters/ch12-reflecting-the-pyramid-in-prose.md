# Reflecting the Pyramid in Prose

> Source: *The Minto Pyramid Principle* by Barbara Minto, OCR pages 222-228.

## Core Idea

Once the pyramid has established what to say and in what order, clear prose comes from visualizing the relationships within each idea and then translating that image into words. The resulting sentence lets the reader reconstruct, understand, and remember the same relationship.

## Frameworks Introduced

### CREATE THE IMAGE

**When to use:** Before drafting a sentence, or whenever prose feels abstract, impenetrable, or difficult to remember.

**How to apply:**

1. Identify the people, objects, events, or concepts being discussed.
2. Build a **memory image** as the information unfolds.
3. Represent abstract ideas with a skeletal structure rather than a detailed scene.
4. Reduce the structure to simple forms—lines, circles, rectangles, or similar shapes.
5. Add arrows or positioning to show direction, sequence, interaction, or comparison.
6. Check whether the image captures the central relationship without unnecessary detail.

**Why it works:** Readers process prose word by word but can hold only a limited number of items in mind. An image compresses many details into a single configuration that is easier to manipulate and recall.

**Failure mode:** If the reader cannot “see” any structure, words accumulate without forming a coherent whole. Earlier parts of a long sentence disappear from memory before its main subject or relationship becomes clear.

### COPY THE IMAGE IN WORDS

**When to use:** To convert a clear thought into a concise sentence, especially when revising jargon-heavy business prose.

**How to apply:**

1. Find the principal nouns in the sentence.
2. Ask how the things named by those nouns relate to one another.
3. Sketch or imagine that relationship.
4. Determine the actual message revealed by the sketch.
5. State that message directly in ordinary English.
6. Verify that the new sentence allows a reader to reconstruct the same image.

**Why it works:** Once the relationship is visible, the writer no longer needs layers of nominalizations, qualifications, or technical-sounding abstractions. The sentence can directly express who or what acts on whom or what.

**Failure mode:** A diagram may expose uncertainty rather than solve it. If a phrase such as “information not adequately considered” is itself vague, the writer must clarify the intended information rather than invent precision.

## Key Concepts

- **Two-step writing process:** First decide the point to make; then put it into words. Pyramid construction completes the first step by establishing both ideas and order.
- **Conceptual thinking in images:** Abstract thinking works by compressing many facts into configurations that can be handled as single units.
- **Memory image:** A mental representation assembled piece by piece while reading; it summarizes the information without preserving the exact wording.
- **Skeletal structure:** A minimal visual representation of abstract relationships, using only enough shape and direction to show how ideas connect.
- **Constructive reading:** The reader actively rebuilds the situation or relationship from successive phrases and sentences.
- **Image-based recall:** Readers may forget or embellish details, but the reconstructed image provides a usable record of the passage.
- **Concrete nouns:** The people, objects, or concepts that give the mind something stable to visualize.
- **Relationship:** The comparison, movement, interaction, sequence, or dependency among the things named in a sentence.
- **Technical language:** Specialist terminology that may be necessary for a specialist audience; it is distinct from needless jargon and cramped style.
- **Lucid prose:** Language from which the reader can straightforwardly reconstruct the writer’s intended image and reasoning.

## Mental Models

- **Use a memory image when successive words are overloading short-term attention.**
- **Use nouns and their relationships when a sentence is buried under abstractions.**
- **Think of an abstract idea as a small diagram, not as a detailed photograph.**
- **Think of writing as sending an ordered structure through a word-by-word channel that the reader must reconstruct.**

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating jargon as evidence of authority:** Technical-sounding language may conceal rather than strengthen an idea. Necessary specialist terms do not justify needless abstraction.
- **Nominalization piles:** Phrases built from words such as “improvement,” “deployment,” “adjustment,” and “requirements” provide no clear actors or relationships.
- **Delayed concrete subjects:** If the meaningful noun arrives late, the reader may have forgotten the opening abstractions before discovering what the sentence concerns.
- **No visualizable relationship:** Listing concepts without showing how they interact leaves the mind “groping” for a structure.
- **Making the wrong thing responsible:** Saying that a system is “not prepared” can attribute a human condition to a mechanism and obscure the actual deficiency.
- **Overdrawing the image:** A complete photographic representation adds effort without aiding reasoning. Only the structural relationship is needed.
- **Rewriting beyond the evidence:** Visualization can reveal missing information, but it cannot determine what an unclear author meant.

## Worked Example

### Before: abstract structure

A system faces demanding accuracy requirements for cash-flow analysis, and improvement is available by incorporating information not adequately considered in projections.

### Extract the nouns

- cash-flow analyses  
- system  
- information  
- improved accuracy  

### Reconstruct the relationship

```text
Required information
        ↓
      SYSTEM
        ↓
Accurate cash-flow analyses
```

The image reveals a simple input-output claim: the analysis becomes accurate when the system receives the necessary information.

### After: direct prose

**The system can produce accurate cash-flow analyses if it receives the required information.**

This revision clarifies the relationship without pretending to know exactly what the missing information is. That detail must come from the original author.

## Key Takeaways

1. Complete the thinking first: establish the pyramid’s points and their order before polishing sentences.
2. Before writing, visualize the people, objects, and concepts involved and how they relate.
3. For abstract material, use a minimal diagram of shapes, lines, and arrows.
4. When revising bad prose, find the nouns and reconstruct their underlying relationship.
5. Translate the resulting image into a direct sentence that the reader can easily rebuild.
6. Retain necessary technical vocabulary, but remove fashionable jargon and tortuous phrasing.
7. Treat an inability to draw the relationship as evidence that the thought—or the source information—may still be unclear.
