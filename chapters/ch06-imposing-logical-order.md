# Imposing Logical Order

> Source: *The Minto Pyramid Principle* by Barbara Minto, OCR pages 93-113.

## Core Idea

Ideas in any grouping must appear in an order dictated by the logical analysis that created the grouping. In inductive reasoning, testing for **time order**, **structural order**, or **degree order** reveals whether ideas truly belong together, are complete, and sit at the same level of abstraction.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Three Logical Orders

Use these orders to test and organize any inductive grouping:

1. **Time Order** — reflects determining the causes of an effect.
2. **Structural Order** — reflects dividing a whole into its parts.
3. **Degree Order** — reflects classifying like things by the extent to which they share a defining characteristic.

A grouping may combine orders, but one must provide its backbone. If no order can be found, the grouping is probably invalid, incomplete, or improperly expressed.

### Time Order

**When to use:** For steps, actions, recommendations, objectives, systems, processes, or conclusions derived from an underlying process.

**How to apply:**

1. State the effect the actions are intended to produce.
2. Visualize yourself performing each action.
3. Ask what tangible result you would have after each action.
4. Distinguish actions from their effects.
5. Put necessary actions in the sequence in which they must occur.
6. Separate mini-processes from the overall process so all points remain at the same level of abstraction.
7. Check whether the reconstructed process exposes omitted steps.

**Why it works:** Actions grouped to achieve an effect form a process, and a process unfolds over time. Reconstructing it exposes mixed levels, missing actions, and conclusions masquerading as steps.

**Common failure mode:** Listing causes and effects together—for example, combining actions taken by a company with later stages of market response.

### Structural Order

**When to use:** When describing, analyzing, creating, or changing a real or conceptual whole—such as an organization, industry, map, machine, financial relationship, or operating system.

**How to apply:**

1. Visualize the whole as a diagram, map, drawing, or conceptual structure.
2. Divide it into parts that are **MECE**.
3. Order the parts as they appear or function within the structure.
4. If describing a structure, proceed in a consistent path, such as top-to-bottom or left-to-right.
5. If recommending changes, present them in the order in which you would build the new structure for the reader.
6. Map loose observations onto the structure to expose gaps or unsupported claims.

**Why it works:** The structure defines both the universe of relevant points and their relationships. Imposing it makes overlaps, omissions, and disconnected assertions visible.

**Common failure mode:** Treating all observations about a structure as a flat list, even though some refer to the whole while others refer to particular parts.

### MECE

**Exact name:** **Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive (MECE)**

**When to use:** Whenever dividing a whole into parts.

**Criteria:**

- **Mutually exclusive:** No part overlaps or duplicates another.
- **Collectively exhaustive:** Together, the parts account for the whole; nothing relevant is omitted.

MECE provides the completeness test underlying structural analysis.

### Degree Order

Also called **comparative order** or **order of importance**.

**When to use:** For a true class of like items—such as problems, reasons, factors, or variables—that share one precisely defined characteristic.

**How to apply:**

1. Define the characteristic common to every item.
2. Separate those items from the larger universe of possible items.
3. Confirm that all known items possessing the characteristic have been included.
4. Rank them by the degree to which each possesses that characteristic.
5. Usually place the strongest item first.

Reversing the ranking for dramatic effect is a stylistic choice, not a logical one.

### Process for Clarifying Improper Class Groupings

Use this procedure when a long list labeled “problems,” “reasons,” or a similar plural noun has no evident order:

1. **Determine what kind of claim each point makes.**
2. **Put claims of the same kind into a common group.**
3. **Infer the natural order from the resulting groups.**

The resulting order may depend on the question being answered—for example, why a failure occurs, why users dislike it, or how it should be fixed.

## Key Concepts

- **Logical order rule:** Ideas in any grouping must be arranged according to their intrinsic logical relationship.
- **Source of the grouping:** The analytical activity—process, structure, or classification—that caused the mind to bring the ideas together.
- **Cause-and-effect hierarchy:** In a long process, one action’s effect may become the input or objective of another action.
- **Same level of abstraction:** Grouped points should be comparable; an overall outcome should not sit beside the substeps that produce it.
- **Underlying process:** An unstated sequence from which a set of conclusions has been derived.
- **Mini-process:** A lower-level sequence embedded within a larger process.
- **Principle of division:** The basis used to split a whole, such as activity, location, or product/market.
- **Proper class:** A grouping whose members share a specifically defined characteristic.
- **Strongest-first order:** The normal presentation of degree-ordered ideas according to relative weight.
- **Magic Number Seven:** A reminder that large groupings are hard to grasp; Minto recommends limiting most groups to four or five because larger lists usually contain closer subgroupings.

## Mental Models

- **Use time order when the ideas collectively produce an effect.**
- **Use structural order when the ideas are parts of something you can visualize.**
- **Use degree order when every item shares one defining characteristic but possesses it to a different extent.**
- **Think of ordering as a diagnostic test: inability to impose an order signals faulty grouping, missing ideas, or mixed abstraction levels.**

## Anti-patterns

- **Grouping by a plural noun alone:** Calling items “five problems” does not establish an internal relationship.
- **Mixing causes with effects:** The reader cannot see what must be done versus what will happen as a result.
- **Mixing abstraction levels:** High-level outcomes placed beside detailed activities conceal the real process.
- **Using long, flat lists:** Closely related ideas remain hidden, so the writer fails to communicate the subgrouping insight.
- **Assuming every list of reasons is degree-ordered:** Items called “reasons” may actually derive from a process or structure.
- **Failing to define the class characteristic:** Without it, mutual exclusivity, completeness, and ranking cannot be tested.
- **Choosing order for drama before logic:** Emotional impact may be stylistically valid, but it does not establish the grouping’s reasoning.
- **Ignoring the question being answered:** The same facts may require different orders depending on whether the purpose is diagnosis, user explanation, or corrective action.

## Worked Example

### Before: a flat list

Users complain that a monthly sales-and-stock report:

- arrives at the wrong frequency,
- contains unreliable inventory figures,
- arrives too late,
- cannot reconcile inventory with sales,
- includes meaningless data,
- has a poor layout,
- does not highlight exceptions,
- requires manual calculations.

### Reconstruct the logic

Classify each complaint by type:

| Type | Complaints |
|---|---|
| Timing | Wrong frequency; arrives too late |
| Data | Unreliable figures; no sales reconciliation; meaningless data |
| Format | Poor layout; no exception highlighting; manual calculations |

Then choose an order based on the question:

- **Why is the report useless?** Data → format → timing.
- **Why do users dislike it?** It arrives late → they cannot find information → what they find is unreliable.
- **How should it be fixed?** Define data and layout → ensure reliability → issue it on time.

### After: answer to the first question

**The system produces a useless monthly report because it:**

1. contains unreliable data,
2. presents the data in an unwieldy format,
3. issues the report too late for practical action.

The original eight complaints become three ordered causes supporting a meaningful conclusion.

## Key Takeaways

- Search every inductive grouping for time, structural, or degree order.
- Reconstruct the process or structure that generated an unclear list.
- Keep grouped points at the same level of abstraction.
- Use MECE whenever dividing a whole into parts.
- Break long lists into meaningful subgroups, preferably no more than four or five points.
- Treat failure to find an order as evidence that the thinking needs correction.
- Impose structure before judging validity; only then can omissions and weak support be seen.
