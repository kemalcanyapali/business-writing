# Structuring the Analysis of the Problem

> Source: *The Minto Pyramid Principle* by Barbara Minto, OCR pages 162-185.

## Core Idea

Structure the problem before gathering data. Diagnostic frameworks identify likely causes and convert them into focused yes-no questions; logic trees then generate and assess possible actions, making both the analysis and its eventual pyramid structure more efficient.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Diagnostic Frameworks

**When to use:** Before fact gathering, especially when locating where a problem lies and why it exists.

**How to use:**

1. Start with the **Opening Scene or Starting Point** of the Problem-Definition framework.
2. Visualize the structure, system, activities, or relationships within which the problem occurred.
3. Develop likely possible causes through division, cause-and-effect tracing, classification, or a combination.
4. Make the upper branches **mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive (MECE)**.
5. Translate each possible cause into a yes-no question.
6. Specify what evidence would answer each question.
7. Test causes in an efficient order, eliminating unlikely or easily disproved causes first.
8. Gather only the data needed to prove or disprove the hypotheses.

**Why it works:** The framework plays the role of the scientific method’s “crucial experiment”: each answer identifies or excludes a cause and indicates when research is complete.

**Failure mode:** Gathering available data first produces numerous interesting but marginal facts, leaves important evidence missing, and makes synthesis into conclusions unnecessarily difficult.

### Showing Physical Structure

**When to use:** When the business or industry contains identifiable units organized into a system—for example, a production process, distribution chain, or marketing system.

**How to use:**

1. Draw the system as it currently operates or ideally should operate.
2. Identify its units, flows, interfaces, and intended function.
3. Locate points where the observed result could be produced.
4. Formulate yes-no questions about each point.
5. Compare the actual structure with the ideal structure to expose weaknesses.

A physical structure can also reveal industry segments, value added, cost behavior, profit sources, asset commitments, and points of leverage.

### Tracing Cause and Effect

**When to use:** When an end result can be decomposed into the financial elements, tasks, or activities that produce it.

#### Financial Structure

Break a result such as return on investment into its financial components, add numbers, and determine whether the problem lies in sales, costs, assets, or their underlying drivers. Continue decomposing until each branch can be tested with a yes-no question.

#### Task Structure

Restate financial components as discrete managerial tasks—for example, “Increase Net Sales” or “Reduce Direct Labor.” Impose the Profit and Loss Account and Balance Sheet on the structure, then analyze trends, sensitivities, and comparisons to set task priorities.

**Why it works:** If a weak point is found, the required direction of action is already explicit.

#### Activity Structure

Begin with an undesirable effect, such as excessive installation time. At each level:

1. Hypothesize all MECE reasons the effect could occur.
2. Break each reason into its possible causes.
3. Continue asking why until the branches identify specific areas for fact gathering.
4. Use subject knowledge to determine where to investigate first.

### Classifying Possible Causes

**When to use:** When likely causes can be grouped by similarity and those groups will help synthesize the evidence.

**How to use:**

1. Create a MECE classification at the upper branch.
2. Generate possible causes within each category.
3. State the evidence needed to prove or eliminate each cause.
4. Convert the branches into yes-no questions.

### Choice Structure

**When to use:** To diagnose an undesirable result by following a sequential process through a series of dual choices.

**How to use:**

1. Visualize the actual sequence of activities.
2. Split each stage into two alternatives.
3. Continue bifurcating until the likely cause becomes precise.
4. Analyze the branches in process order.

A **sequential marketing structure** is a more sophisticated form. It analyzes positioning, distribution, awareness, trial, and repurchase in sequence. Weaknesses on the left must be corrected before those on the right.

### Logic Trees

**When to use:** After determining where the problem lies and why it exists, to answer:

- What could we do about it?
- What should we do about it?

**How to use:**

1. State the action objective.
2. Decompose all logically possible actions into MECE alternatives.
3. Continue to actionable choices.
4. Estimate the benefit and risk of each action.
5. Select the recommended set.

Logic trees can also expose gaps, overlaps, and irrelevant items in an existing grouping.

### Issue Analysis

Strictly, an **issue** is a question requiring a yes-or-no answer. Use Issue Analysis to bring analysis to a clear decision point, not as a loose label for concerns, topics, or every kind of logic tree.

## Key Concepts

- **Abduction:** Generating likely possible explanations for why a problem exists, then testing them.
- **Opening Scene or Starting Point:** The portion of the Problem-Definition framework whose structure implies the diagnostic frameworks needed.
- **R1:** The present, undesirable result delivered by the existing system.
- **R2:** The desired result the system should deliver.
- **Hypothesis:** A probable explanation to be proved or disproved through focused evidence.
- **MECE:** Mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive branches covering the relevant possibilities without overlap.
- **Issue:** A question phrased to demand a yes-or-no answer.
- **Concern:** A topic indicating what worries the client but not yet developed into a testable issue.
- **Point of leverage:** A place in a business or industry structure where focused change may have significant effect.
- **Crucial experiment:** A test whose possible outcomes exclude one or more hypotheses.

## Mental Models

- **Use a diagnostic framework when asking where the problem lies and why it exists.**
- **Use a logic tree when asking what actions are possible and which should be taken.**
- **Think of yes-no questions as research completion criteria: they define both the evidence required and when to stop.**
- **Think of the Opening Scene as the map from which the appropriate analytical structure should be derived.**

## Anti-patterns

- **Starting with available data:** Produces excessive facts, wasted analysis, missing evidence, and last-minute research.
- **Organizing findings by broad topics:** Headings such as Operations, Marketing, or Issues do not reveal a logical relationship or support clear conclusions.
- **Using “Findings, Conclusions, Recommendations” as the analytical structure:** This reflects work sequence rather than the logic of the problem.
- **Calling open questions issues:** “How should we reorganize?” is a concern; “Should we reorganize functionally?” is an issue.
- **Confusing diagnostic frameworks with decision trees or PERT diagrams:** Diagnostic frameworks generate questions about causes; decision trees and PERT diagrams reveal decisions, actions, or activity dependencies.
- **Investigating every branch equally:** Subject knowledge should guide the order of testing and prevent unnecessary work.
- **Treating analysis as abstract logic:** Frameworks cannot substitute for extensive, accessible knowledge of the field.

## Worked Example

A distributor’s new owners believe that **$31 million of inventory is too high**.

### Before: loosely grouped “key issues”

- What inventory investment is necessary?
- Is centralization cost-effective?
- What are obsolete-stock levels?
- How much can inventory turns improve?
- Is the current management system suitable?

These questions mix concerns, causes, methods, and outcomes.

### After: problem-led structure

**R1:** Excessive capital tied up in inventory  
**R2:** The right amount of capital devoted to inventory

First determine whether the present level is actually above the amount required to meet customer-service objectives. If yes, decompose the excess:

```text
Excess inventory cost
├── Order too much
└── Keep inventory too long
```

This produces testable issues:

1. **Is the centralized management system placing orders improperly?**
2. **Is it retaining excessive obsolete or slow-moving inventory?**

The study process then becomes coherent:

```text
Determine required service-level inventory
→ Test whether purchasing procedures can lower it
→ Test whether decentralizing the structure can lower it further
```

The issues, analytical process, and intended study outputs now align.

## Key Takeaways

- Build the diagnostic framework before launching broad interviews or data collection.
- Derive likely causes from the structure of the Opening Scene, not from whatever data happen to be available.
- Turn every causal branch into a clear yes-no question and specify the evidence required.
- Use physical, financial, task, activity, classification, or choice structures according to the nature of the problem.
- Diagnose causes before using logic trees to generate solutions.
- Validate written groupings by mapping them back to the analytical structure that produced them.
- Use subject expertise to prioritize tests and develop creative recommendations.
